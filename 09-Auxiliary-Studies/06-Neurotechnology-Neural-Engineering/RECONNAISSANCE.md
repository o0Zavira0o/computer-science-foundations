# Reconnaissance — Neurotechnology & Neural Engineering

**Track:** `neurotechnology-neural-engineering` (`NNE`)
**Baseline audit date:** 2026-08-26

## Entry model

The track is designed for zero subject-specific knowledge. It does not assume a prior neuroscience, physiology, circuits, signal-processing, machine-learning, or medical-device course.

The curriculum therefore begins below the usual university neural-engineering prerequisite line and explicitly builds the biological and engineering vocabulary needed to reason about neural interfaces.

## Why both “neurotechnology” and “neural engineering”

The two labels overlap but emphasize different viewpoints.

- **Neurotechnology** is the broader family of tools and systems used to observe, map, analyze, influence, restore, or interact with nervous-system structure and function.
- **Neural engineering** emphasizes the engineering discipline required to model, design, build, test, and translate those interfaces and systems.

The track keeps both names because a mature curriculum needs both the technology landscape and the engineering method.

## Baseline sources

The curriculum was cross-checked against:

- University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign's current Neural Engineering program and NE course catalog for an academic curriculum baseline;
- NIH BRAIN Initiative strategy and current Neural Recording and Modulation program for research scope across electrical, optical, magnetic, acoustic, molecular, computational, and integrated technologies;
- OpenStax for accessible neurophysiology foundations;
- FDA neurological-device resources and implanted-BCI guidance for device safety, reliability, testing, clinical, and regulatory context;
- NIH BRAIN neuroethics guidance for privacy, consent, agency, identity, equity, and responsible innovation;
- a foundational BCI review defining BCIs as systems that measure CNS activity and translate it into artificial outputs.

## Coverage decisions

The graph deliberately progresses through:

1. field map and nervous-system foundations;
2. neural signals and measurement theory;
3. invasive and noninvasive recording modalities;
4. electrodes, instrumentation, sampling, noise, and artifacts;
5. neural data, coding, decoding, encoding, and evaluation;
6. stimulation and modulation;
7. BCI, neuroprosthetics, and closed-loop systems;
8. implants, materials, tissue response, packaging, power, telemetry, and reliability;
9. peripheral interfaces, tissue engineering, and in-vitro platforms;
10. translation, regulation, human factors, cybersecurity, and neuroethics;
11. advanced integrated systems and research literacy.

This ordering is intentional. It prevents a common failure mode in neurotechnology education: jumping directly to a fashionable device or ML model without understanding what biological signal exists, how the interface transforms it, what artifacts enter the measurement, or what safety and human constraints govern the system.

## Core design principle

Every advanced lesson should be able to answer five questions:

1. **Biological source or target:** what tissue, cell population, circuit, or physiological process is involved?
2. **Interface physics:** how does the device sense or perturb it?
3. **Signal/computation:** what transformations occur between raw interaction and interpreted output?
4. **System objective:** observation, diagnosis, restoration, rehabilitation, therapy, research, or augmentation?
5. **Constraints:** selectivity, resolution, bandwidth, invasiveness, stability, safety, latency, usability, regulation, and ethics?

A lesson that cannot locate itself in that system model is not yet mature enough for this track.

## Visual policy

Use selective diagrams when geometry or physical arrangement materially changes understanding: neuron/membrane anatomy, electrode placement, volume conduction, recording scales, stimulation fields, closed-loop architectures, implant stacks, and prosthetic feedback loops.

Do not use brain imagery as decoration.

## Safety policy

The track explains mechanisms, engineering principles, evidence, and design tradeoffs. It does not provide self-experimentation protocols, surgical instructions, individualized treatment recommendations, or unsupervised stimulation settings.

## Frontier policy

Neural-interface hardware, clinical evidence, FDA guidance, standards, high-channel-count systems, closed-loop algorithms, foundation models, privacy practice, and neuroethics are version-sensitive. Refresh authoritative and primary sources before making current-status claims in advanced lessons.
