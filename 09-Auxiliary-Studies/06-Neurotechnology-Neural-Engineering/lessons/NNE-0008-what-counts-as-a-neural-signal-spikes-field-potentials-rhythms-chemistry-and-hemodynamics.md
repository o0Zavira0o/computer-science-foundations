# What counts as a neural signal: spikes, field potentials, rhythms, chemistry, and hemodynamics

## If you landed here directly

This lesson assumes the scale ladder from `NNE-0007`.

You should already be comfortable with:

- membrane voltage and action potentials;
- synaptic currents and postsynaptic potentials;
- single neurons, populations, circuits, systems, and behavior;
- the distinction between biological activity and the subset sampled by an instrument.

Now we ask a deceptively simple question:

> when a neural engineer says "signal," what physical quantity is actually changing?

The answer depends on the modality.

A "neural signal" may be:

- a membrane-voltage event;
- an extracellular voltage generated by many transmembrane currents;
- a spike train extracted from extracellular voltage;
- a rhythmic pattern computed from an electrical recording;
- a concentration of a neurochemical;
- a hemodynamic variable coupled indirectly to neural metabolism;
- a peripheral neural or muscular electrical signal.

These are not interchangeable.

By the end, you should be able to:

- distinguish biological source, transduced quantity, and recorded signal;
- explain what spikes, local field potentials, ECoG, and EEG represent at a first-principles level;
- explain why a rhythm or frequency band is usually a pattern extracted from a time series rather than a new physical substance;
- distinguish electrical, chemical, and hemodynamic measurements;
- explain why BOLD fMRI is an indirect proxy for neural activity;
- compare rough temporal and spatial scales without treating them as universal constants;
- explain where EMG and peripheral nerve signals fit in neural engineering;
- identify common interpretation errors caused by mixing measurement scales;
- prepare for the measurement-chain lesson that follows.

---

## The problem worth understanding

Suppose four laboratories say:

> "We measured neural activity."

Lab A records extracellular spikes with a microelectrode.

Lab B records scalp EEG.

Lab C measures extracellular dopamine with electrochemistry.

Lab D collects BOLD fMRI.

All four may be studying the nervous system.

But they are not measuring the same physical variable.

Their raw measurements differ in:

- physical origin;
- spatial scale;
- temporal scale;
- invasiveness;
- selectivity;
- preprocessing;
- relationship to neural computation.

So the phrase:

```text
neural activity
```

is too broad to interpret a dataset.

The first question must be:

> what quantity did the sensor actually respond to?

---

## Signal is a relationship, not a magic substance

In engineering, a **signal** is a varying quantity used to represent or carry information about a system.

Examples:

```text
voltage versus time
current versus time
chemical concentration versus time
optical intensity versus time
magnetic-field estimate versus time
```

A neural signal is therefore not one universal biological object.

It is a measured or modeled quantity connected to nervous-system activity.

---

## Biological source versus measured signal

Keep three layers separate:

```text
biological event
→ physical consequence
→ measurement
```

Example:

```text
synaptic currents
→ extracellular electric field
→ voltage measured by electrode
```

Another:

```text
neural activity
→ metabolic / vascular response
→ BOLD contrast change
```

The sensor does not necessarily measure the biological event directly.

---

## Direct does not mean perfect

A microelectrode may measure extracellular voltage generated very near neurons.

That is relatively direct compared with hemodynamics.

But even then:

- electrode geometry matters;
- tissue conductivity matters;
- distance matters;
- multiple sources contribute;
- filtering matters.

"Direct" is relative.

No instrument gives an interpretation-free view of the nervous system.

---

## Proxy

A **proxy** is a measurable quantity used as evidence about another quantity.

Examples:

```text
BOLD signal
→ proxy related to neural activity through neurovascular coupling

surface EMG
→ proxy related to aggregate muscle-fiber activation

EEG voltage
→ proxy related to organized population current sources
```

A good proxy can be highly useful without being identical to the underlying process.

---

## Signal modality

A **modality** is a measurement approach with a characteristic physical basis.

At L0, useful broad groups are:

```text
electrical
chemical
hemodynamic
optical / magnetic and other derived modalities
```

This lesson concentrates on the first three and on rhythms extracted from time series.

---

# Part I — Spikes

## Intracellular action potential

Inside a neuron, the action potential is a rapid change in membrane voltage.

Conceptually:

```text
V_m(t)
```

changes from resting values through:

- depolarization;
- overshoot;
- repolarization;
- recovery.

That is the cellular event learned in `NNE-0005`.

---

## Extracellular spike

An extracellular electrode does not normally record a scaled copy of the intracellular membrane voltage.

Instead, it measures extracellular voltage created by nearby transmembrane currents.

When an action potential propagates near the electrode, the extracellular waveform can appear as a brief spike-like deflection.

So:

```text
intracellular action potential
≠
extracellular spike waveform
```

They are linked physical events observed from different geometries.

---

## Extracellular spike polarity is not a universal biological sign

A waveform may have:

- negative phase;
- positive phase;
- biphasic or triphasic shape.

The exact waveform depends on:

- electrode position;
- cell geometry;
- current-source distribution;
- reference configuration;
- filtering.

Do not interpret:

```text
negative extracellular spike
```

as:

```text
neuron became electrically negative inside
```

The geometries are different.

---

## Single-unit activity

A recording may isolate waveform events attributed to one neuron or one putative neuron.

This is often called **single-unit activity**.

The phrase describes an analysis target.

It does not guarantee perfect biological identity.

Spike sorting can make errors.

---

## Multi-unit activity

When activity from several nearby neurons is not separated into unique units, the result can be summarized as **multi-unit activity**.

This samples local spiking at a coarser cellular identity level.

Again:

```text
single-unit
multi-unit
```

are analysis/measurement categories, not different types of action potential biology.

---

## Spike train

After detecting spike times, a spike train can be represented as events:

```text
t1, t2, t3, ...
```

or mathematically as impulses:

$$ s(t)=\sum_k \delta(t-t_k). $$

This is a representation.

The real biological action potential has finite waveform duration.

---

## Firing-rate estimate

A firing rate can be estimated from a spike train by:

- counting spikes in windows;
- smoothing event times;
- estimating an instantaneous rate model.

So:

```text
spike train
→ processing
→ firing-rate estimate
```

The rate is derived from the event sequence.

It is not a separate sensor measurement.

---

## Example NNE-EX-036 — one neuron, three representations

Suppose a neuron fires at:

```text
100 ms
145 ms
210 ms
```

Three representations are possible.

### Raw extracellular waveform

```text
voltage samples versus time
```

### Detected spike train

```text
[100, 145, 210] ms
```

### Smoothed firing-rate estimate

```text
r(t)
```

These are related.

But they are not identical datasets.

Each processing step discards or transforms information.

---

## Spikes have excellent temporal precision

Action potentials occur on millisecond timescales.

Extracellular spike recording can therefore capture rapid event timing.

But high temporal precision does not automatically mean:

- full circuit coverage;
- good access to every neuron;
- perfect cell-type identity.

Temporal resolution and biological coverage are separate dimensions.

---

# Part II — Field potentials

## What is an extracellular field?

Neuronal membrane currents create electric potentials in conductive tissue.

An electrode placed in extracellular space measures a voltage relative to a reference.

Many sources can contribute.

This creates an extracellular field signal.

---

## Local field potential

A **local field potential**, or LFP, is an extracellular electrical signal recorded within neural tissue, typically emphasizing slower components than sorted spikes.

It reflects local aggregate transmembrane-current activity.

Important contributors can include:

- synaptic currents;
- return currents;
- intrinsic membrane currents;
- synchronized population activity.

Do not define LFP as:

> the average firing rate around the electrode.

That is not generally correct.

---

## Spikes and LFP can coexist on one electrode

One extracellular microelectrode can record a broadband voltage.

Processing may separate:

```text
higher-frequency, brief components
→ spike-related analysis

lower-frequency field components
→ LFP analysis
```

The split depends on the acquisition and filtering pipeline.

The biological sources overlap in space but are not identical.

---

## Field potential is a mixture

At an electrode:

```text
measured voltage
=
superposition of contributions from many sources
+
noise
+
reference effects
```

The electrode sees a weighted mixture.

Weights depend on geometry and tissue.

This is the beginning of the **volume-conduction** problem.

---

## Volume conduction

Electrical potentials spread through conductive biological tissue.

Therefore an electrode can detect sources located away from its exact physical contact point.

This is useful.

It also creates ambiguity.

A signal at one electrode is not automatically generated directly beneath that electrode.

---

## ECoG

**Electrocorticography**, or ECoG, records electrical potentials from electrodes placed on or near the cortical surface.

Compared with scalp EEG, ECoG is:

- closer to cortical sources;
- more invasive;
- generally capable of finer spatial and higher-frequency access.

But it still records aggregate fields.

It is not a grid of direct single-neuron voltmeters.

---

## EEG

**Electroencephalography**, or EEG, records electrical potential differences at the scalp.

The signal reflects large-scale organized neural current sources after propagation through:

- brain tissue;
- cerebrospinal fluid;
- skull;
- scalp.

Therefore scalp EEG is a spatially mixed population-level signal.

---

## Why aligned populations matter for EEG

A large macroscopic scalp potential requires source currents that combine coherently enough to survive distance and volume conduction.

Cortical geometry and synchronized transmembrane currents matter.

A single neuron's extracellular field is far too small to dominate scalp EEG.

---

## EEG is not "brain electricity leaving the skull"

The scalp electrodes measure voltage differences produced by internal current-source distributions through conductive tissue.

Nothing needs to "leak out" as a stream of electrical substance.

This is field measurement.

---

## Reference electrode matters

Electrical recordings measure potential differences.

The chosen reference affects the recorded waveform.

Therefore:

```text
channel signal
```

often means:

```text
electrode potential relative to reference scheme
```

Different referencing choices can change apparent amplitude and spatial pattern.

---

## Common average and bipolar references

Examples of reference strategies include:

- dedicated reference electrode;
- common average;
- bipolar derivations.

Each emphasizes different spatial relationships.

Reference choice is part of signal definition.

---

## Example NNE-EX-037 — same cortical event at three electrical scales

Imagine synchronized synaptic activity in a cortical patch.

### Intracortical electrode

May observe:

- local field components;
- nearby spikes.

### ECoG electrode

May observe a broader cortical field.

### Scalp EEG electrode

May observe a further spatially mixed and attenuated field contribution.

Same underlying circuit event.

Different measured signals because:

```text
sensor location
+
source geometry
+
volume conduction
+
reference
+
filtering
```

change the observation.

---

# Part III — Rhythms and frequency-domain descriptions

## A rhythm is usually a pattern in a signal

Suppose EEG voltage oscillates approximately periodically.

We may describe a rhythm by:

- frequency;
- amplitude;
- phase;
- duration;
- spatial distribution.

The rhythm is a temporal pattern in the measured signal.

It is not usually a separate physical substance.

---

## Frequency

Frequency describes how rapidly a periodic component repeats.

Unit:

```text
hertz
=
cycles per second
```

For a period `T`:

$$ f=\frac{1}{T}. $$

A 10 Hz rhythm has a period of approximately:

$$ T=0.1\ \text{s}. $$

---

## Oscillation does not require a perfect sine wave

Real neural signals are:

- noisy;
- transient;
- nonstationary;
- mixtures of sources.

A rhythm may appear only briefly.

Frequency-domain methods can still summarize repeating structure.

---

## Frequency band

Researchers often group frequencies into named bands.

Examples include labels such as:

- delta;
- theta;
- alpha;
- beta;
- gamma.

Band boundaries vary across fields and studies.

Do not treat them as natural constants with universal meanings.

---

## Band label is not a mechanism

A spectral peak near 10 Hz does not by itself tell you:

- which neurons generated it;
- which synapses caused it;
- what cognitive process it represents.

Frequency is a descriptive feature.

Mechanistic interpretation requires additional evidence.

---

## Power spectrum

A power spectrum summarizes how signal power is distributed across frequency.

Conceptually:

```text
time-domain waveform
→ spectral analysis
→ power versus frequency
```

This is a derived representation.

The sensor still measured voltage versus time.

---

## Phase

Phase describes position within an oscillatory cycle.

Two signals can have:

- same frequency;
- different phase.

Phase relationships can be used to study coordination.

But observed phase depends on filtering, reference, conduction, and analysis choices.

---

## Synchrony versus oscillation

Two neurons can synchronize without producing a perfectly periodic rhythm.

A population can oscillate without every neuron firing on every cycle.

So:

```text
synchrony
≠
identical periodic spiking
```

---

## Rhythms can emerge from circuits

Recurrent excitation and inhibition can support rhythmic dynamics.

But a rhythm observed in EEG or LFP is the measurement of a population-level field pattern.

Do not jump directly from:

```text
spectral peak
```

to:

```text
one known microcircuit mechanism
```

without evidence.

---

## Filtering can create misleading confidence

Band-pass filtering can make a noisy signal appear more oscillatory.

This does not mean filtering invented all structure.

But it means:

> analysis choices shape what is visible.

Always inspect:

- raw signal;
- filter settings;
- edge effects;
- artifact sources.

---

# Part IV — Chemical neural signals

## Neural communication is also chemical

Synapses release neurotransmitters.

Neuromodulators and metabolites change over time.

Therefore nervous-system signaling includes chemical variables such as:

```text
dopamine concentration
glutamate concentration
serotonin concentration
oxygen
glucose
ions
```

depending on the experiment.

---

## Chemical measurement asks a different question

An electrode measuring extracellular voltage asks:

> what electric potential exists here relative to a reference?

A neurochemical sensor asks:

> how much of a target chemical species is present or changing here?

Different physical quantity.

Different selectivity problem.

---

## Electrochemical sensing

Some neurochemicals can be measured electrochemically.

For example, fast-scan cyclic voltammetry can detect rapid changes in electroactive species such as dopamine under appropriate conditions.

The measured current depends on electrochemical reactions at the electrode.

So the raw signal is not literally:

```text
dopamine molecules per second
```

Calibration and analysis connect current to concentration estimates.

---

## Selectivity

A chemical sensor must distinguish the target analyte from interfering species.

This is a major challenge.

Electrical voltage recording and chemical sensing therefore face different ambiguity types.

Electrical:

```text
which sources contributed to this field?
```

Chemical:

```text
which analyte generated this sensor response?
```

---

## Sensitivity

**Sensitivity** describes how strongly the sensor output changes with the quantity being measured.

For chemical sensors, weak concentrations require enough response to distinguish signal from noise.

Sensitivity and selectivity are not the same.

A sensor can be sensitive but nonspecific.

---

## Microdialysis

**Microdialysis** samples extracellular molecules through a semipermeable membrane.

The collected fluid can be analyzed chemically.

It can provide strong chemical specificity.

But sampling and diffusion can limit temporal resolution compared with fast electrical measurements.

---

## Tradeoff: chemistry versus timing

A simplified contrast:

```text
electrical spike:
very fast timing
limited chemical identity

microdialysis:
strong chemical information
slower temporal sampling

fast electrochemistry:
faster chemical dynamics
target/selectivity constraints
```

No modality wins every dimension.

---

## Example NNE-EX-038 — dopamine transient versus spike train

Suppose during a reward task:

```text
spike rate rises within tens of milliseconds
dopamine concentration rises over a different time course
```

These signals may be related.

But they are not duplicates.

The spike train measures electrical events from sampled neurons.

The dopamine sensor measures a chemical concentration-related signal in extracellular space.

Both contribute different information about the circuit.

---

## Chemical diffusion matters

After release, molecules:

- diffuse;
- bind receptors;
- undergo reuptake;
- degrade or metabolize.

Therefore chemical signals have spatial and temporal dynamics different from membrane voltage.

---

## Sensor chemistry is part of the measurement chain

For a neurochemical recording:

```text
target molecule
→ electrode / probe interaction
→ electrical or optical transduction
→ amplifier / instrument
→ digitized signal
```

The next lesson will formalize this measurement-chain view.

---

# Part V — Hemodynamic signals

## Neural activity consumes energy

Neural activity requires:

- ATP;
- oxygen;
- glucose supply.

The vascular system responds to local metabolic demand through neurovascular coupling.

This creates measurable hemodynamic consequences.

---

## Hemodynamics

**Hemodynamics** concerns blood flow, blood volume, and blood oxygenation.

These variables can change after neural activity.

They are not membrane voltages.

They are physiological responses coupled to neural metabolism and vascular regulation.

---

## BOLD fMRI

BOLD means:

```text
blood-oxygen-level dependent
```

BOLD fMRI is sensitive to changes related to the balance of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin.

Neural activity can trigger vascular responses that alter this balance.

Therefore BOLD provides an indirect measure related to neural activity.

---

## BOLD is not a direct spike detector

A BOLD voxel does not count action potentials.

The chain is closer to:

```text
neural activity
→ metabolic demand
→ neurovascular response
→ blood oxygenation / flow changes
→ MR contrast change
```

Several biological transformations occur before the measured signal.

---

## Hemodynamic response is slower

Neuronal spikes occur on millisecond scales.

Hemodynamic responses evolve over seconds.

Therefore fMRI can provide excellent spatial information while having much slower temporal dynamics than electrophysiology.

This is a modality tradeoff.

---

## Spatial resolution is not source specificity

A small fMRI voxel does not imply:

```text
one cell type
one synapse type
one unique computation
```

A voxel contains many:

- neurons;
- glia;
- vessels;
- synapses.

Good spatial localization is not equivalent to microscopic biological specificity.

---

## Neurovascular coupling can vary

The relationship between neural activity and hemodynamics can depend on:

- brain region;
- vascular state;
- baseline physiology;
- task;
- disease;
- drugs.

So BOLD interpretation relies on a coupling model.

---

## Example NNE-EX-039 — same event in electrophysiology and BOLD

Suppose a cortical population becomes strongly active for one second.

### Electrode

May show changes almost immediately in:

- spikes;
- field potentials.

### BOLD fMRI

May show a delayed hemodynamic response over seconds.

The measurements differ because:

```text
electrode:
electrical consequence

fMRI:
vascular / oxygenation consequence
```

The delay is not evidence that the brain "processed the event seconds later."

It is partly measurement physiology.

---

## fNIRS preview

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy can estimate changes related to oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin optically.

Like BOLD, it is hemodynamic.

It is not a direct electrical measurement.

Different instrumentation.

Related physiological proxy.

---

# Part VI — Peripheral signals: ENG and EMG

## Neural engineering extends beyond the brain

Neurotechnology includes peripheral interfaces.

Useful electrical signals include:

- ENG;
- EMG.

They belong in the broader nervous-system measurement landscape.

---

## ENG

**Electroneurography**, or ENG, records electrical activity associated with peripheral nerves.

Depending on geometry and technique, one may observe compound activity generated by many axons.

This is different from scalp EEG and different from intracellular single-neuron recording.

---

## Compound action potential

When many axons activate together, their extracellular contributions can combine into a compound action potential.

The waveform depends on:

- axon conduction velocities;
- recruitment;
- electrode geometry;
- distance.

A compound potential is a population signal.

---

## EMG

**Electromyography**, or EMG, measures electrical activity associated with muscle activation.

The signal arises from muscle-fiber action potentials driven by motor-unit activity.

EMG is therefore not literally a brain signal.

But it is highly relevant to neural engineering because it reflects motor-system output.

---

## Surface versus intramuscular EMG

Surface EMG records aggregate activity through the skin.

Intramuscular electrodes can access more localized motor-unit activity.

Again:

```text
same physiological system
different sampling geometry
different signal
```

---

## EMG can be control input

Myoelectric prostheses can use EMG features to estimate intended movement.

That does not mean:

```text
EMG = intention
```

It means EMG contains predictive information about motor commands and muscle activation.

---

# Part VII — Modality tradeoff space

## Every modality trades dimensions

Useful dimensions include:

- temporal resolution;
- spatial resolution;
- invasiveness;
- biological specificity;
- coverage;
- stability;
- signal-to-noise ratio;
- hardware complexity;
- portability.

There is no universally best neural signal.

---

## Temporal resolution

Rough hierarchy:

```text
spikes / electrophysiology:
milliseconds

many chemical methods:
milliseconds to minutes depending on method

hemodynamics:
seconds-scale response
```

These are broad categories.

Exact values depend on system and instrument.

---

## Spatial scale

Roughly:

```text
intracellular / single-unit:
cellular or near-cellular sampling

LFP:
local population field

ECoG:
mesoscopic cortical surface field

EEG:
large-scale scalp field

fMRI:
voxel-scale hemodynamic map
```

Do not turn this into a rigid universal ranking.

---

## Coverage versus specificity

A microelectrode can provide highly local detail.

EEG can cover much of the head noninvasively.

These strengths are different.

```text
local specificity
versus
large-scale coverage
```

is a common tradeoff.

---

## Invasiveness

Examples:

```text
intracellular recording:
highly invasive

intracortical electrode:
invasive

ECoG:
invasive

EEG:
noninvasive

fMRI:
noninvasive measurement
```

But "noninvasive" does not mean:

- cheap;
- portable;
- high temporal resolution.

---

## Signal amplitude is not scientific importance

A large-amplitude signal is not necessarily:

- more informative;
- more causal;
- more specific.

Amplitude depends on sensor physics and preprocessing.

Compare signals only within a meaningful calibration and modality context.

---

## Sampling rate is not biological bandwidth

Recording at:

```text
30 kHz
```

does not mean the biological process contains 30,000 independent meaningful events per second.

Sampling rate is an acquisition choice.

Biological bandwidth is a property of the signal source and measurement chain.

---

## Artifact

An **artifact** is a measured component caused by something other than the target biological process.

Examples:

- movement;
- mains interference;
- electrode motion;
- eye movements in EEG;
- muscle activity contaminating EEG;
- scanner motion in fMRI;
- stimulation artifact.

Artifacts can be larger than the neural signal of interest.

---

## Artifact versus noise

Noise is often random or unwanted variability.

Artifact often has a specific non-target source.

The boundary is context dependent.

Both can distort interpretation.

---

## Example NNE-EX-040 — choose a modality from the question

Suppose four research questions:

### Question A

> When does one identified neuron fire relative to a stimulus?

Best-fit family:

```text
single-unit electrophysiology
```

### Question B

> How does a distributed cortical rhythm change across sleep stages?

Possible family:

```text
EEG
```

### Question C

> How does extracellular dopamine change after reward delivery?

Possible family:

```text
neurochemical sensing
```

### Question D

> Which large-scale brain regions show task-related hemodynamic changes?

Possible family:

```text
fMRI
```

The modality should follow the scientific question.

Not the reverse.

---

# Part VIII — A signal is produced by a measurement chain

## The raw signal is already transformed

Consider EEG.

Before the digitized samples exist:

```text
neural transmembrane currents
→ volume-conducted scalp potentials
→ electrode interface
→ amplifier
→ analog filter
→ ADC
→ digital samples
```

Every stage influences the observed signal.

---

## Sensor transfer function

A sensor and front-end do not pass every frequency equally.

They have a **transfer function**.

Some components are:

- attenuated;
- amplified;
- phase shifted;
- saturated.

Therefore the recorded waveform is shaped by instrumentation.

---

## Dynamic range

A front-end can only represent a finite amplitude range.

If the input exceeds it:

```text
saturation / clipping
```

can occur.

Clipping is not a biological plateau.

It is an instrument limitation.

---

## Resolution

Digitization maps continuous voltage to discrete numerical levels.

Bit depth affects amplitude resolution.

This is separate from:

- spatial resolution;
- temporal sampling rate.

The word "resolution" needs a qualifier.

---

## Time alignment

Comparing modalities requires considering delays.

Example:

```text
spike time:
near-immediate electrical event

BOLD:
delayed vascular response
```

Cross-modal synchronization must account for physiology and instrumentation.

---

## Common failure mode: all neural signals are electrical

No.

Neural engineering also measures:

- chemicals;
- hemodynamics;
- optical proxies;
- magnetic fields.

---

## Common failure mode: EEG is the average of spikes

No.

EEG is a large-scale extracellular field measurement dominated by organized population current sources and volume conduction.

---

## Common failure mode: LFP is just low-pass spike rate

No.

LFP is an extracellular potential arising from transmembrane currents.

Spike rates and LFP can correlate, but they are different observables.

---

## Common failure mode: a rhythm is a new physical signal source

Usually the rhythm is a pattern or feature of a measured time series.

The physical source still needs explanation.

---

## Common failure mode: gamma means one universal cognitive process

No.

Frequency-band labels are descriptive and context dependent.

---

## Common failure mode: fMRI measures neurons firing

No.

BOLD fMRI measures hemodynamic contrast related indirectly to neural activity.

---

## Common failure mode: better spatial resolution means better cellular specificity

No.

A small voxel or electrode footprint can still contain many biological sources.

---

## Common failure mode: high sampling rate means high information rate

No.

Sampling rate and biological information content are different.

---

## Common failure mode: chemical sensor current equals concentration without calibration

No.

Sensor response must be related to analyte concentration through calibration and selectivity assumptions.

---

## Common failure mode: surface EMG is a cortical signal

No.

It reflects electrical activity of muscle fibers downstream of motor-system commands.

---

## Common failure mode: one electrode equals one source

No.

Most extracellular measurements are mixtures.

---

## Common failure mode: noninvasive means low-risk interpretation

Noninvasive describes physical access, not inferential certainty.

A noninvasive signal can still be difficult to interpret mechanistically.

## Connection to Linear Algebra

Suppose 64 EEG channels are sampled at 1000 time points.

One representation is a matrix:

```text
channels × time
```

Each row and column has semantics.

The matrix does not remove the physics.

It organizes the measurements.

This is why `LA-0009` emphasized matrix shape and meaning.

---

## Connection to matrix-vector multiplication

A linear spatial filter can combine multiple channels:

```text
channel vector
→ weighted combination
→ output feature
```

This is exactly the type of operation introduced in `LA-0010`.

But a linear combination is an analysis operation.

It does not imply the biological sources were themselves linearly separable.
