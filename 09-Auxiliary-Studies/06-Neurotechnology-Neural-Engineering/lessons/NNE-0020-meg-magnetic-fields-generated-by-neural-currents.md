# MEG: magnetic fields generated by neural currents

## If you landed here directly

The direct prerequisite is:

`NNE-0019 — Peripheral neural and neuromuscular signals: ENG and EMG`.

This lesson also relies heavily on two earlier ideas:

```text
NNE-0015
extracellular population fields

NNE-0018
sensor space is not source space
```

The new measurement modality is magnetoencephalography:

```text
MEG
=
magnetoencephalography
```

The sentence to keep throughout this lesson is:

> MEG measures extremely weak magnetic fields outside the head that are generated by neural electric currents; the sensors do not directly measure membrane voltage, a cortical image, or a uniquely localized source.

A second sentence is equally important:

> MEG and EEG observe related electromagnetic consequences of neural current flow, but they are not interchangeable because electric potentials and magnetic fields depend differently on source orientation, head geometry, sensor geometry, and reference conventions.

---

# Part I — Start from the physical question

Every neural current creates an electromagnetic field.

Earlier lessons focused mostly on electric potential:

```text
neural current
→ extracellular electric field
→ voltage difference
```

MEG asks a different question:

```text
neural current
→ magnetic field outside the head
→ magnetic sensor
```

This changes the sensor technology and some aspects of source sensitivity.

It does not remove the inverse problem.

---

# Part II — Electric current and magnetic field are linked

For a small current element, the magnetic contribution can be represented by the Biot–Savart relation:

$$ d\mathbf{B}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} $$

The equation tells us four qualitative facts:

- current produces magnetic field;
- field direction depends on current direction;
- field direction also depends on observation direction;
- field magnitude falls rapidly with distance.

For neural populations, the geometry is much more complicated than one wire segment.

But the directional logic survives.

---

# Part III — What quantity does MEG measure?

MEG sensors measure components of the magnetic flux density:

```text
B
```

whose SI unit is:

```text
tesla
T
```

Useful scales include:

```text
1 pT = 10^-12 T
1 fT = 10^-15 T
```

Neural MEG fields are commonly in the femtotesla-to-picotesla range.

That is extraordinarily small.

---

# Part IV — Worked example NNE-EX-094: the scale problem

Take a simplified comparison:

```text
Earth's magnetic field:
50 μT

brain MEG signal:
100 fT
```

Convert:

$$ 50\ \mu\mathrm{T}=50\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{T} $$

and:

$$ 100\ \mathrm{fT}=100\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{T}=10^{-13}\ \mathrm{T} $$

The ratio is:

$$ \frac{50\times10^{-6}}{10^{-13}}=5\times10^8 $$

So the background Earth field in this example is roughly:

```text
500,000,000 times larger
```

than the neural signal.

This is why MEG instrumentation is fundamentally an exercise in:

```text
extreme sensitivity
+
interference control
```

not merely "putting a magnetic detector near the head."

---

# Part V — The dominant biological source is population current

A single neuron usually produces a field far too small to detect noninvasively outside the head.

MEG becomes measurable when many neurons contribute coherently.

The important contributors are typically:

```text
synchronized transmembrane synaptic currents
+
intracellular axial currents
+
associated return currents
```

in populations of aligned cortical neurons.

As in EEG:

```text
population geometry
+
synchrony
+
orientation
+
cancellation
```

matter.

---

# Part VI — Why cortical pyramidal cells are important

Cortical pyramidal neurons have approximately aligned apical dendrites within a local cortical patch.

When many such neurons receive synchronized synaptic input:

```text
aligned dendritic current flow
→
macroscopic current pattern
→
measurable external electromagnetic field
```

The same broad population physiology contributes to both EEG and MEG.

But the two modalities weight the resulting fields differently.

---

# Part VII — MEG is not primarily a direct action-potential detector

Action potentials are electrically real and generate magnetic fields.

But conventional noninvasive MEG is dominated by slower synchronized postsynaptic and intracellular current patterns from neuronal populations.

Why?

Because:

- postsynaptic currents can persist longer;
- many neighboring neurons can align and synchronize;
- geometry favors spatial summation at macroscopic scale.

Therefore:

```text
MEG waveform
≠
one axonal spike waveform enlarged
```

---

# Part VIII — The equivalent current dipole

A sufficiently compact active cortical patch can be approximated by an **equivalent current dipole**.

We can denote its dipole moment by:

```text
q
```

with units:

```text
A·m
```

Conceptually:

```text
many microscopic neuronal currents
→
one effective source vector
```

The vector encodes:

- source location;
- source orientation;
- source strength.

It is a model.

It is not a literal anatomical object.

---

# Part IX — Current dipole versus magnetic dipole

Do not confuse:

```text
equivalent current dipole
```

with:

```text
magnetic dipole
```

MEG source models commonly describe an effective **electric current dipole** inside the brain.

The magnetic field is generated by that current configuration.

---

# Part X — The field circles around current

A current direction defines a surrounding magnetic-field pattern.

The right-hand rule provides the basic intuition:

```text
thumb
→ current direction

curled fingers
→ magnetic field circulation
```

That does not mean MEG sensors literally trace perfect circles.

Real neural sources and head geometry produce distributed field patterns.

# Part XII — Source orientation matters strongly

Imagine a cortical current dipole.

Rotate it while holding location and magnitude fixed.

The external magnetic-field pattern changes.

Therefore:

```text
same source strength
+
different source orientation
→
different sensor pattern
```

This is one reason a MEG amplitude cannot be interpreted without geometry.

---

# Part XIII — Tangential and radial orientation

In an ideal spherical conductor:

```text
tangential cortical current dipole
→
strong external MEG field

purely radial current dipole
→
zero external MEG field in the ideal model
```

This result is central to classical MEG intuition.

But the real head is not a perfect sphere.

So the safer practical statement is:

> MEG is generally much less sensitive to radial cortical source components than to tangential ones, rather than universally incapable of detecting every source with a radial component.

---

# Part XIV — Gyri and sulci create orientation structure

Cortical pyramidal cells are oriented approximately normal to the local cortical surface.

Therefore:

```text
gyral crown
→ often more radial relative to head

sulcal wall
→ often more tangential relative to head
```

This creates an anatomical sensitivity bias.

Do not translate it into:

```text
MEG only sees sulci
```

Real cortical folding, head shape, mixed source orientations, and modern arrays make the situation more nuanced.

---

# Part XV — Worked example NNE-EX-095: orientation without activity change

Suppose two equally strong cortical patches activate.

Patch A is oriented mainly tangentially.

Patch B is oriented mainly radially with respect to an ideal spherical head.

A conventional external MEG array could show:

```text
Patch A
→ strong field pattern

Patch B
→ very weak or nearly absent pattern
```

The incorrect conclusion would be:

> Patch B had little neural activity.

The correct conclusion is:

> measured field strength reflects both source activity and source orientation.

---

# Part XVI — Primary and volume currents

Neural activation produces:

```text
primary current
```

associated with active cellular current flow.

It also drives:

```text
volume currents
```

through surrounding conductive tissue.

Both are part of the electromagnetic forward problem.

MEG is often described as being less affected than EEG by conductivity boundaries in the skull and scalp.

That statement is useful.

But:

```text
less affected
≠
unaffected
```

---

# Part XVII — Why skull conductivity affects EEG and MEG differently

Scalp EEG measures electric potential differences.

Those potentials depend strongly on how currents spread through tissues with different conductivity.

MEG measures magnetic fields from current flow.

Under common head-model assumptions, the skull's low electrical conductivity distorts the magnetic field less than it distorts scalp potential.

So MEG often preserves source spatial structure differently from EEG.

But head geometry and conductivity still enter accurate MEG forward models.

---

# Part XVIII — MEG does not "look through the skull" like a camera

A common phrase says:

```text
magnetic fields pass through the skull
```

This can mislead.

The skull is not an optical window.

MEG measures a field outside the head that is generated by internal currents.

The sensor never sees the neurons directly.

The inference still requires:

```text
electromagnetic model
+
head geometry
+
sensor geometry
+
source assumptions
```

---

# Part XIX — The MEG sensor array

A conventional whole-head MEG system places many magnetic sensors around the head.

Each sensor measures a component of the field.

Therefore:

```text
one sensor channel
=
one spatial projection of the total field
```

not:

```text
one sensor
=
one cortical region
```

This is exactly the sensor-space/source-space distinction from EEG.

---

# Part XX — Sensor orientation matters too

A magnetic sensor is sensitive along one or more defined axes.

For a single-axis sensor:

```text
measured value
=
component of B along sensor axis
```

If the true field vector is orthogonal to that axis, the sensor can read very little even when the local total field is not small.

Thus:

```text
source orientation
+
sensor position
+
sensor axis
```

jointly determine the measurement.

---

# Part XXI — Magnetometers

A magnetometer measures magnetic field at one location.

Conceptually:

$$ y_i(t)=\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}_i,t) $$

where:

- $\mathbf{r}_i$ is sensor location;
- $\mathbf{u}_i$ is sensitive-axis direction.

A magnetometer is sensitive to both:

```text
brain fields
and
environmental fields
```

which is useful and dangerous.

---

# Part XXII — Gradiometers

A gradiometer measures a spatial difference in magnetic field.

A simple idealized first-order gradiometer can be written:

$$ y(t)=B(\mathbf{r}_1,t)-B(\mathbf{r}_2,t) $$

If a distant environmental field is almost identical at the two pickup locations:

```text
environment
→ largely cancels
```

while a nearby brain field with stronger spatial variation can remain.

---

# Part XXIII — Worked example NNE-EX-096: why a gradiometer rejects distant interference

Suppose:

```text
sensor 1:
environment = 10,000 pT
brain = 0.10 pT

sensor 2:
environment = 10,000 pT
brain = 0.07 pT
```

The magnetometer-like readings are dominated by environment.

But the ideal difference is:

$$ (10000+0.10)-(10000+0.07)=0.03\ \mathrm{pT} $$

The huge common environmental term cancels.

This is the principle.

Real gradiometers have finite baselines, calibration errors, orientation details, and nonuniform interference.

---

# Part XXIV — Why a magnetically shielded room is used

The brain field is tiny.

Sources of magnetic contamination include:

- elevators;
- vehicles;
- electrical infrastructure;
- moving ferromagnetic objects;
- power systems;
- nearby equipment;
- Earth's field interacting with motion.

A magnetically shielded room reduces external field penetration.

It does not create perfect zero field.

---

# Part XXV — Passive and active shielding

Interference control can use:

```text
passive shielding
```

with high-permeability and conductive layers,

plus:

```text
active compensation
```

using field sensors and compensation coils.

Modern OPM systems particularly emphasize active field control because sensor dynamic range and participant motion can be limiting.

---

# Part XXVI — Conventional MEG and SQUIDs

Traditional high-performance MEG systems commonly use:

```text
SQUID
=
superconducting quantum interference device
```

SQUID systems can detect extraordinarily small magnetic flux changes.

But superconductivity requires cryogenic operation.

That hardware requirement shapes the entire scanner.

---

# Part XXVII — Cryogenics creates a stand-off distance

The cold SQUID sensors must be thermally isolated from the participant.

Therefore conventional systems place sensors inside a rigid dewar.

The scalp cannot touch the sensors directly.

This creates:

```text
sensor-to-scalp distance
```

that reduces signal strength.

It also creates fit problems for smaller heads.

---

# Part XXVIII — A rigid helmet is not equally optimal for every head

A fixed helmet must accommodate many participants.

If the head is smaller:

```text
larger air gap
→
larger source-to-sensor distance
→
weaker fields at sensors
```

Coverage can also become less uniform.

This is especially important in pediatric MEG.

---

# Part XXIX — Signal strength falls rapidly with distance

For a compact current source, the external magnetic field decreases strongly with source-to-sensor distance.

The precise scaling depends on source model and geometry.

So avoid treating one power law as universal.

The engineering conclusion is robust:

```text
closer sensor
→ usually stronger brain signal
```

if other factors remain comparable.

---

# Part XXX — OPM-MEG

An optically pumped magnetometer uses atomic physics rather than superconductivity to measure magnetic field.

OPM sensors can operate without cryogenic cooling.

This enables:

```text
sensor closer to scalp
+
head-conforming arrays
+
wearable configurations
```

which can improve sensitivity and movement tolerance.

---

# Part XXXI — OPM-MEG is not simply "SQUID without helium"

Changing the sensor changes system constraints.

OPMs introduce challenges involving:

- background field magnitude;
- field gradients;
- sensor calibration;
- cross-axis response;
- movement through residual magnetic fields;
- thermal and mechanical placement;
- active shielding.

So:

```text
new sensor
→
new system design
```

not merely a drop-in replacement.

---

# Part XXXII — Conventional and OPM MEG are one modality, different instrumentation

Both aim to estimate:

```text
magnetic field generated by neural currents
```

The distinction is primarily in sensing technology and geometry.

Therefore:

```text
SQUID-MEG
and
OPM-MEG
```

share the same core source physics.

---

# Part XXXIII — MEG is reference-free in a specific sense

EEG channels are voltage differences.

A channel requires a reference or a differential montage.

MEG magnetometers measure magnetic field components directly.

So conventional MEG does not require a voltage reference electrode.

But this does **not** mean:

```text
MEG has no spatial comparison or calibration problem
```

Gradiometers use differences.

Arrays require calibration.

Source reconstruction still depends on geometry.

---

# Part XXXIV — Sensor-space topography

At one time point, the sensor array produces a spatial field pattern.

This can be plotted over the sensor helmet.

Such a map is:

```text
MEG sensor-space field map
```

It is not automatically:

```text
cortical activation map
```

The distinction is the same one learned in scalp EEG.

---

# Part XXXV — Dipolar field patterns

A compact cortical source often generates a characteristic external pattern with:

```text
positive field region
+
negative field region
```

across sensors.

The zero crossing and extrema are useful geometric clues.

But:

```text
field maximum
≠
source directly underneath
```

---

# Part XXXVI — Why the source is not under the maximum sensor

The sensor extrema are consequences of the entire dipolar field geometry.

For a tangential current dipole, the magnetic field emerges and re-enters around the source region.

The source is geometrically related to the spatial pattern.

It is not simply located at the largest sensor value.

---

# Part XXXVII — Head position is part of the data

The MEG helmet is fixed in scanner coordinates.

The participant's head can occupy different positions inside it.

Therefore:

```text
sensor coordinates
≠
head coordinates automatically
```

A localization pipeline must know the relative geometry.

---

# Part XXXVIII — Head-position indicator coils

Many conventional systems use small localization coils attached to the head.

Their known electromagnetic signals allow the system to estimate head position relative to the sensor array.

This supports:

- initial localization;
- movement tracking;
- movement compensation.

The coils are not neural sensors.

---

# Part XXXIX — Digitized head shape and landmarks

Coregistration commonly uses:

- fiducial landmarks;
- head-surface points;
- head-position coils;
- anatomical MRI.

The goal is to establish transformations between coordinate systems.

A typical chain is:

```text
MEG sensor coordinates
↔
head coordinates
↔
MRI coordinates
```

---

# Part XL — Coregistration error becomes source error

If the head model is misaligned with the sensor array:

```text
wrong source-sensor geometry
→
wrong lead field
→
biased source estimate
```

This is why digitization and coregistration are not administrative details.

They are part of the forward model.

---

# Part XLI — The MEG forward problem

For a chosen source model and head geometry, the forward problem asks:

> If the source currents were known, what magnetic field would each sensor measure?

A linear model can be written:

$$ \mathbf{b}(t)=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{j}(t)+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t) $$

where:

- $\mathbf{b}(t)$ is sensor data;
- $\mathbf{j}(t)$ is source current parameters;
- $\mathbf{L}$ is the lead-field matrix;
- $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)$ is noise and unmodeled activity.

---

# Part XLII — What the lead field contains

The lead field depends on:

- source location;
- source orientation;
- sensor location;
- sensor orientation;
- head geometry;
- conductivity model;
- sensor type.

Therefore:

```text
lead field
=
measurement geometry encoded as a linear mapping
```

It is not the measured neural data itself.

---

# Part XLIII — Forward problem versus inverse problem

The forward problem:

```text
source
→
sensor field
```

is well-defined once the model is specified.

The inverse problem:

```text
sensor field
→
source
```

is not uniquely determined by measurements alone.

This asymmetry is fundamental.

---

# Part XLIV — The MEG inverse problem

The inverse problem asks:

> Which internal current distribution generated the measured sensor field?

There are usually more possible source configurations than independent sensor measurements.

Different source configurations can generate identical or nearly identical external fields.

Therefore:

```text
MEG source localization
requires constraints
```

---

# Part XLV — Worked example NNE-EX-097: the null-space problem

Suppose:

$$ \mathbf{b}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{j}_1 $$

and there exists a nonzero vector $\mathbf{n}$ such that:

$$ \mathbf{L}\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{0} $$

Define:

$$ \mathbf{j}_2=\mathbf{j}_1+\mathbf{n} $$

Then:

$$ \mathbf{L}\mathbf{j}_2=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{j}_1+\mathbf{L}\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{b} $$

So two different source vectors produce exactly the same measured field.

That is not a software bug.

It is mathematical nonuniqueness.

---

# Part XLVI — Source localization is model-based inference

To obtain one useful estimate, algorithms impose structure.

Examples include:

- one or a few equivalent current dipoles;
- minimum-norm distributed current;
- beamformers;
- Bayesian priors;
- anatomical orientation constraints.

Each method asks a different constrained inverse question.

---

# Part XLVII — Equivalent current dipole fitting

ECD fitting assumes a compact source.

The algorithm searches for:

- location;
- orientation;
- amplitude;

whose predicted field best matches the observed field.

This can be powerful for focal responses.

It can be misleading for distributed or multiple simultaneous sources.

---

# Part XLVIII — Goodness of fit is not biological truth

A dipole can explain a high fraction of sensor variance.

That means:

```text
the dipole model reproduces the measured field well
```

It does not prove:

```text
one tiny cortical patch was the only active generator
```

Multiple biological configurations can project similarly.

---

# Part XLIX — Distributed source estimates

Distributed methods place many candidate sources across cortex or volume.

They then choose a solution using regularization or priors.

Advantages:

- no need to assume one focal source;
- can represent extended activity.

Limitations:

- spatial leakage;
- smoothing;
- depth bias;
- dependence on regularization;
- nonuniqueness remains.

---

# Part L — Minimum-norm intuition

A minimum-norm solution prefers a source pattern with small overall norm while fitting the data.

This turns:

```text
infinitely many possible sources
```

into:

```text
one regularized estimate
```

The result is useful precisely because a prior was added.

So never call it assumption-free.

---

# Part LI — Beamforming intuition

A beamformer constructs a spatial filter for a candidate source location.

Conceptually:

```text
combine many sensors
→
pass activity consistent with one location/orientation
→
suppress activity from elsewhere
```

The weights depend on:

- lead field;
- data covariance;
- regularization.

Correlated sources can create failure modes.

---

# Part LII — Source leakage

A reconstructed source time series can contain contributions from nearby or even distant true sources.

This is often called:

```text
spatial leakage
```

or related terms depending on the method.

Therefore:

```text
source-space signal
≠
perfectly isolated biological region
```

---

# Part LIII — Connectivity is especially vulnerable

Suppose one real source projects to two reconstructed locations.

The two estimated time series can become correlated even if there are not two interacting biological generators.

Therefore:

```text
estimated connectivity
can be inflated by
field spread + inverse leakage
```

This is a major interpretive boundary.

---

# Part LIV — Worked example NNE-EX-098: common-source correlation

Suppose one real neural process is $s(t)$.

Two sensor channels are:

$$ y_1(t)=s(t)+n_1(t) $$

and:

$$ y_2(t)=0.8s(t)+n_2(t) $$

Even if there are no two interacting neural populations, $y_1$ and $y_2$ can be strongly correlated because they share the same source.

Therefore:

```text
sensor correlation
≠
neural connectivity by itself
```

The same logic can persist after imperfect source reconstruction.

---

# Part LV — MEG spatial resolution is not one fixed number

You will sometimes hear:

```text
MEG spatial resolution = a few millimeters
```

or:

```text
MEG spatial resolution = centimeters
```

Both can be contextually defensible.

Neither is universally correct.

Localization accuracy depends on:

- SNR;
- source depth;
- source extent;
- number of simultaneous sources;
- sensor geometry;
- head model;
- coregistration;
- inverse method;
- prior assumptions.

---

# Part LVI — Temporal resolution is a genuine strength

MEG tracks electromagnetic consequences of neural current flow directly enough to preserve millisecond-scale timing.

This makes it well suited for studying:

- sensory evoked responses;
- transient cortical events;
- oscillations;
- rapid task dynamics;
- propagation timing.

But:

```text
millisecond sampling
≠
millimeter certainty
```

Temporal and spatial inference are different axes.

---

# Part LVII — Sampling still sets a digital limit

The underlying field changes continuously.

The acquisition system samples it.

Therefore:

```text
sensor physics
+
analog bandwidth
+
sampling rate
+
filtering
```

determine what temporal detail reaches the file.

Formal sampling and aliasing belong to `NNE-N-0023`.

---

# Part LVIII — Evoked fields

If the same stimulus is repeated:

```text
stimulus
→
time-locked neural response
→
time-locked magnetic field
```

trials can be aligned and averaged.

The averaged result is often called an:

```text
evoked field
```

The averaging emphasizes phase-locked activity.

---

# Part LIX — Averaging is not a neutral operation

Averaging can improve SNR for reproducible time-locked responses.

But it suppresses activity that:

- changes latency;
- changes phase;
- occurs only in some trials;
- is not time-locked.

So:

```text
evoked response
≠
all task-related neural activity
```

---

# Part LX — Induced oscillatory responses

A task can change oscillatory power without preserving identical phase across trials.

If you average the raw waveform first:

```text
phase-varying oscillation
→
can cancel
```

Yet time-frequency analysis can reveal a consistent power change.

Detailed spectra and time-frequency analysis belong to `NNE-N-0028`.

---

# Part LXI — MEG and EEG observe related sources

Both modalities are generated by neural electrical currents.

Both are especially sensitive to synchronized population activity.

Both can resolve millisecond dynamics.

Both face a nonunique inverse problem.

So:

```text
MEG and EEG
=
complementary electromagnetic measurements
```

---

# Part LXII — What EEG measures

Scalp EEG measures:

```text
electric potential difference
```

between electrodes.

Its channel definition depends on reference or montage.

The scalp potentials are shaped strongly by volume conduction through brain, CSF, skull, and scalp.

---

# Part LXIII — What MEG measures

MEG measures:

```text
magnetic field component
```

at external sensor locations.

It has no EEG-style voltage reference electrode.

It is often less distorted by skull conductivity boundaries.

But it is more strongly orientation-selective in characteristic ways.

---

# Part LXIV — MEG and EEG sensitivity are complementary

A source weakly visible in MEG may be clearer in EEG.

A source spatially blurred in EEG may have a more distinctive MEG field pattern.

Combined interpretation can improve confidence because the modalities impose different spatial weighting on related neural currents.

Therefore:

```text
MEG versus EEG
```

should not be framed as:

```text
which one is universally better?
```

---

# Part LXV — A practical comparison

| Property | Scalp EEG | MEG |
|---|---|---|
| measured quantity | voltage difference | magnetic field component |
| sensor | electrode | SQUID / OPM / related magnetic sensor |
| reference electrode | required for voltage definition | no EEG-style voltage reference |
| dominant population source | synchronized neural currents | synchronized neural currents |
| skull conductivity influence | strong | generally weaker |
| radial-source sensitivity | substantial | reduced in classical spherical intuition |
| tangential-source sensitivity | substantial | strong |
| environmental electromagnetic interference | important | extremely important |
| temporal resolution | millisecond-scale | millisecond-scale |
| source inverse problem | nonunique | nonunique |
| structural anatomy | often MRI-assisted | often MRI-assisted |

---

# Part LXVI — MEG is not MRI

MEG and MRI both contain the word:

```text
magnetic
```

but they measure different physics.

MRI:

```text
strong static magnetic field
+
radiofrequency excitation
+
nuclear magnetic resonance
→
anatomical / physiological image
```

MEG:

```text
passive detection of tiny magnetic fields
generated by neural electric currents
```

They are separate modalities.

---

# Part LXVII — Why MRI is often used with MEG anyway

MEG provides functional electromagnetic data.

MRI provides detailed anatomy.

Combining them supports:

```text
source estimate
+
anatomical location
```

This is often called magnetic source imaging in clinical contexts.

But the MRI did not generate the MEG waveform.

---

# Part LXVIII — MEG versus fMRI

The next canonical lesson is:

`NNE-N-0021 — fMRI and the BOLD signal: hemodynamic inference rather than direct voltage recording`.

MEG:

```text
electrophysiological current
→
magnetic field
→
sensor
```

fMRI BOLD:

```text
neural activity
→
metabolic / vascular response
→
blood oxygenation change
→
MR signal
```

This difference explains the major temporal-resolution contrast.

---

# Part LXIX — "Direct" still requires careful wording

MEG is often called a direct measure of neural activity compared with hemodynamic imaging.

That is reasonable when contrasting:

```text
electromagnetic field
versus
vascular surrogate
```

But MEG still does not directly read membrane voltage from a neuron.

Better wording:

> MEG is a direct electrophysiological field measurement whose interpretation still requires a forward and inverse model.

---

# Part LXX — Biological artifacts

The brain is not the only biological magnetic source.

Potential contaminants include:

- cardiac magnetic fields;
- eye movement;
- retinal currents;
- facial and neck muscles;
- jaw muscles;
- respiratory movement;
- peripheral muscle activation.

Some are much larger than the cerebral signal of interest.

---

# Part LXXI — Environmental artifacts

A passing vehicle or moving metal object can generate interference far exceeding neural MEG.

Therefore the recorded channel is better thought of as:

$$ y(t)=b_{\mathrm{brain}}(t)+b_{\mathrm{bio}}(t)+b_{\mathrm{environment}}(t)+n(t) $$

The goal is not to pretend only the brain exists.

The goal is to model, prevent, reject, or separate unwanted terms.

---

# Part LXXII — Metal near the participant

Ferromagnetic contamination can produce large artifacts.

Sources can include:

- jewelry;
- clothing hardware;
- dental material;
- implanted or attached devices;
- environmental objects.

Actual participant screening follows facility-specific safety and acquisition protocols.

This lesson does not provide clinical screening instructions.

---

# Part LXXIII — Movement matters

If the head moves inside a fixed SQUID helmet:

```text
same neural source
+
different sensor geometry
→
different measured field
```

Movement can therefore mimic signal change.

It also alters the lead field used for source reconstruction.

---

# Part LXXIV — OPM movement creates a different challenge

Wearable OPM sensors move with the head.

This preserves source-to-sensor geometry better.

But the whole sensor array moves through the residual environmental magnetic field.

Therefore:

```text
motion robustness
requires
field control
```

It is not automatic.

---

# Part LXXV — Noise suppression is part of the measurement system

Common strategies include:

- shielded room;
- gradiometric sensing;
- reference sensors;
- active compensation;
- signal-space separation;
- regression;
- ICA;
- trial rejection.

Each method has assumptions.

Artifact removal can remove brain signal too.

Detailed artifact processing belongs to `NNE-N-0025`.

---

# Part LXXVI — Signal-space separation intuition

A multichannel MEG array measures spatial patterns.

Fields originating inside the sensor array have different spatial structure from many fields originating outside it.

Signal-space separation methods exploit this difference.

This is a model-based separation.

It is not a magical noise eraser.

---

# Part LXXVII — Bad sensors

A sensor can be:

- noisy;
- saturated;
- miscalibrated;
- unstable;
- disconnected.

Bad channels should be identified and documented.

Silently keeping them can distort:

- averages;
- covariance;
- source reconstruction;
- connectivity.

---

# Part LXXVIII — Calibration

A sensor's gain and orientation must be known.

If one sensor is scaled incorrectly:

```text
sensor field map
→ distorted
```

If orientation is wrong:

```text
lead field
→ wrong
```

Array calibration is therefore a physical model issue.

---

# Part LXXIX — Coils and magnetometers are not all identical

A MEG system may contain:

- magnetometers;
- planar gradiometers;
- axial gradiometers;
- OPM axes;
- reference sensors.

Different channel types have different units, sensitivity patterns, and noise properties.

Never merge them blindly as if they were identical scalar channels.

---

# Part LXXX — Units can differ by sensor type

Magnetometer channels commonly use:

```text
tesla
```

Gradiometers measure a field difference over distance.

Their effective units can be represented as:

```text
T/m
```

depending on calibration and system convention.

Therefore numerical amplitudes from different sensor types should not be compared without accounting for units.

---

# Part LXXXI — Source depth matters

A deeper source is farther from the sensors.

Its field is weaker and spatially broader at the helmet.

Therefore:

```text
deep source
→
lower SNR
+
less distinctive spatial pattern
```

All else equal.

MEG is especially effective for many cortical sources.

Subcortical source inference is more challenging, not universally impossible.

---

# Part LXXXII — "MEG cannot see deep brain" is too absolute

Deep structures can contribute measurable signals under favorable conditions.

But:

- distance is larger;
- SNR is lower;
- source geometry may be unfavorable;
- superficial sources can dominate.

So the correct statement is:

> sensitivity and localization performance generally worsen with depth.

---

# Part LXXXIII — Source extent matters

A large cortical patch can contain subregions with different orientations.

Their magnetic contributions can:

- reinforce;
- partially cancel;
- create a field not well fit by one dipole.

Therefore:

```text
large source
≠
necessarily larger simple MEG peak
```

---

# Part LXXXIV — Cancellation occurs in MEG too

Suppose two current patches produce opposing fields at a sensor.

Then:

```text
B1 + B2 ≈ 0
```

The sensor reads little.

That does not mean both sources were inactive.

As in EMG and EEG:

```text
small measured amplitude
≠
small total biological activity
```

---

# Part LXXXV — Sensor count is not source count

A 300-channel MEG system does not imply:

```text
300 independent neural sources
```

Channels are correlated projections of a lower-dimensional external field produced by distributed internal currents.

The inverse problem remains underdetermined.

---

# Part LXXXVI — More sensors help, but geometry matters

Increasing sensor count can improve spatial sampling.

But improvement depends on:

- coverage;
- distance;
- orientation;
- noise;
- calibration;
- source depth;
- redundancy.

Packing more sensors into nearly identical locations yields diminishing returns.

---

# Part LXXXVII — OPM arrays change spatial sampling

Because OPMs can be placed closer to the scalp and in flexible geometry:

```text
same number of sensors
```

can provide different information from a fixed SQUID helmet.

This means:

```text
channel count alone
```

is a poor cross-system comparison.

---

# Part LXXXVIII — Source-space visualization

Source estimates are often plotted on:

- cortical surfaces;
- MRI slices;
- volumetric grids.

These images can look anatomically precise.

But visual precision can exceed inferential precision.

Always ask:

```text
what inverse model?
what regularization?
what head model?
what source orientation constraints?
what uncertainty?
```

---

# Part LXXXIX — A colored cortical map is not raw MEG

Raw MEG exists in sensor space.

The cortical map is a transformation.

The chain is:

```text
sensor data
→
forward model
→
inverse method
→
source estimate
→
visualization
```

Each arrow adds assumptions.

---

# Part XC — Statistical maps add another layer

A source estimate may then be:

- averaged;
- baseline corrected;
- normalized;
- contrasted;
- thresholded;
- statistically tested.

So a final colored brain figure may be several inferential steps removed from the measured magnetic field.

That is normal.

It must be documented.

---

# Part XCI — MEG and clinical epilepsy

One established clinical use is localization of epileptiform activity for presurgical evaluation.

MEG can help estimate the generators of interictal discharges.

But:

```text
MEG spike source
≠
entire epileptogenic network automatically
```

Clinical interpretation integrates multiple data sources.

---

# Part XCII — Functional mapping

MEG can also map:

- somatosensory responses;
- auditory responses;
- language-related activity;
- motor activity.

These maps can support preoperative planning.

Again:

```text
functional localization
=
model-based estimate
```

not direct visualization of firing neurons.

---

# Part XCIII — Frequency-domain MEG

MEG is widely used to study oscillations such as:

- alpha;
- beta;
- gamma;
- task-induced rhythmic changes.

But frequency labels do not uniquely identify mechanism.

Formal spectral reasoning belongs to `NNE-N-0028`.

---

# Part XCIV — Connectivity and causality are different

A correlation, coherence, or phase relation between two MEG signals does not establish causal influence.

Potential confounds include:

- common source;
- field spread;
- leakage;
- common stimulus;
- common reference-like processing;
- filtering;
- volume conduction.

Connectivity interpretation requires explicit method-specific reasoning.

---

# Part XCV — Cross-frequency coupling requires the same caution

Two frequency components can appear statistically coupled.

Possible causes include:

- neural interaction;
- waveform shape;
- harmonics;
- filtering;
- nonstationarity;
- common source.

A coupling metric is not a mechanism detector by itself.

---

# Part XCVI — Beamformer "virtual sensors"

A beamformer can produce a source-space time series for a chosen location.

This is often called a:

```text
virtual sensor
```

It is not a physical sensor.

It is a weighted combination of physical MEG channels.

---

# Part XCVII — Why covariance matters to beamforming

Beamformer weights depend on how channels co-vary.

If covariance is estimated poorly:

```text
spatial filter
→ unstable
```

If true sources are strongly correlated:

```text
beamformer
→ can suppress or distort them
```

Therefore data length and regularization matter.

---

# Part XCVIII — Minimum norm and beamforming answer different questions

Minimum norm asks approximately:

```text
what distributed source pattern
with a chosen norm/prior
best explains the data?
```

Beamforming asks approximately:

```text
what spatial filter
best passes activity from this candidate location
while suppressing other covariance?
```

Neither universally dominates.

---

# Part XCIX — Coordinate systems can silently break analysis

Possible coordinate systems include:

- device coordinates;
- head coordinates;
- MRI voxel coordinates;
- MRI surface coordinates;
- standard template coordinates.

A transformation error can put an otherwise good source estimate in the wrong anatomical location.

Always track coordinate frames.

---

# Part C — Individual MRI versus template MRI

Using an individual structural MRI usually improves anatomical fidelity.

A template can be useful when individual anatomy is unavailable.

But:

```text
template anatomy
→
additional geometric uncertainty
```

especially in atypical head shapes.

---

# Part CI — Conductivity model complexity

MEG is less sensitive than EEG to skull conductivity.

That does not make conductivity irrelevant.

Possible models include:

- sphere;
- overlapping spheres;
- boundary-element methods;
- finite-element methods.

Model choice depends on desired accuracy and application.

---

# Part CII — A spherical model is a model, not a head

Spherical models provide powerful intuition:

- tangential sensitivity;
- simple field geometry;
- analytic solutions.

But real heads contain:

- non-spherical skull;
- CSF;
- cavities;
- varying tissue boundaries.

So use the sphere to reason.

Do not confuse it with anatomy.

---

# Part CIII — Good MEG inference needs explicit uncertainty

Sources of uncertainty include:

- sensor noise;
- coregistration;
- head position;
- source depth;
- head model;
- inverse regularization;
- source extent;
- trial variability.

A source coordinate reported to many decimal places is not necessarily known to that precision.

---

# Part CIV — Precision versus accuracy

If repeated estimates cluster tightly:

```text
high precision
```

But if all are displaced because coregistration is wrong:

```text
low accuracy
```

MEG pipelines need both concepts.

---

# Part CV — Reproducibility requires metadata

A useful MEG dataset or paper should document:

- system model;
- sensor types;
- sampling rate;
- acquisition passband;
- bad channels;
- shielded-room setup;
- head-position method;
- participant position;
- head movement;
- coregistration;
- MRI usage;
- preprocessing order;
- artifact handling;
- forward model;
- inverse method;
- regularization;
- coordinate system.

This anticipates `NNE-N-0029`.

---

# Part CVI — A MEG file is not just a matrix

A raw MEG data matrix needs interpretation metadata.

For example:

```text
channels × time
```

does not tell you:

- which channels are magnetometers;
- which are gradiometers;
- which sensors are bad;
- where the head was;
- how events align;
- which coordinate frame is used.

The metadata is part of the measurement.

---

# Part CVII — Event timing matters

A visual stimulus shown by software at time $t$ may physically appear on the display later.

An auditory stimulus may have hardware delay.

If the goal is millisecond neuroscience:

```text
trigger time
≠
physical stimulus time automatically
```

Good practice can use photodiodes or other timing checks.

---

# Part CVIII — Why MEG rooms are behaviorally constrained

Conventional MEG often requires participants to:

- minimize head motion;
- avoid magnetic objects;
- remain within a fixed helmet;
- perform tasks compatible with the scanner.

These constraints shape experimental design.

OPM-MEG is expanding the possible task space.

---

# Part CIX — Wearable does not mean ordinary environment

An OPM helmet may move with the participant.

But sensitive magnetic measurements still require environmental field control.

So:

```text
wearable MEG
≠
take-home EEG cap
```

at least for current high-performance systems.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
```

# Part CXI — A compact source-model chain

```text
biology
→
current density J(r,t)
→
magnetic field B(r,t)
→
sensor projection
→
channel data
→
source model
```

A later analysis must never jump from:

```text
channel peak
```

directly to:

```text
specific neuron population
```

without the intermediate model.

# Part CXII — Failure mode 1: "MEG measures magnetic activity"

Why it fails:

Neurons generate **electrical currents**.

MEG measures the magnetic fields produced by those currents.

---

# Part CXIII — Failure mode 2: "MEG measures membrane voltage"

Why it fails:

MEG sensors detect external magnetic field, not intracellular voltage.

---

# Part CXIV — Failure mode 3: "One MEG sensor corresponds to one cortical area"

Why it fails:

Every sensor measures a weighted projection from many possible sources.

---

# Part CXV — Failure mode 4: "MEG topography is a brain activation image"

Why it fails:

It is a sensor-space magnetic field map.

---

# Part CXVI — Failure mode 5: "The source is under the largest sensor"

Why it fails:

Dipolar field geometry places extrema away from the source in characteristic patterns.

---

# Part CXVII — Failure mode 6: "MEG is equally sensitive to every source orientation"

Why it fails:

Orientation strongly shapes external field strength.

---

# Part CXVIII — Failure mode 7: "MEG never detects radial sources"

Why it fails:

That is an ideal spherical-model statement taken too literally.

Real head geometry and mixed source orientations complicate it.

---

# Part CXIX — Failure mode 8: "The skull has no effect on MEG"

Why it fails:

MEG is generally less distorted by conductivity boundaries than EEG, but accurate forward models still depend on head geometry and current flow.

---

# Part CXX — Failure mode 9: "MEG has perfect spatial resolution because magnetic fields are not blurred by skull"

Why it fails:

Source localization remains noisy, depth-dependent, model-dependent, and nonunique.

---

# Part CXXI — Failure mode 10: "A 300-channel system gives 300 sources"

Why it fails:

Channels are spatial projections, not independent biological generators.

---

# Part CXXII — Failure mode 11: "MEG is MRI"

Why it fails:

MEG passively measures neural magnetic fields; MRI uses nuclear magnetic resonance in strong externally applied fields.

---

# Part CXXIII — Failure mode 12: "MRI source overlay means MRI measured the neural activity"

Why it fails:

MRI provides anatomy.

MEG provides electromagnetic functional data.

---

# Part CXXIV — Failure mode 13: "Reference-free means geometry-free"

Why it fails:

MEG lacks an EEG-style reference electrode, but sensor calibration, orientation, coordinate systems, gradiometer baselines, and source geometry remain crucial.

---

# Part CXXV — Failure mode 14: "Gradiometers remove all noise"

Why it fails:

They suppress spatially common interference better than local gradients, but real environmental fields are not perfectly uniform.

---

# Part CXXVI — Failure mode 15: "Shielded room means zero environmental field"

Why it fails:

Residual fields remain and can interact with motion or equipment.

---

# Part CXXVII — Failure mode 16: "SQUID is the source of the brain signal"

Why it fails:

The SQUID is a detector.

The source is neural current.

---

# Part CXXVIII — Failure mode 17: "OPM-MEG changes the underlying neural physics"

Why it fails:

The sensor technology changes.

The field source remains neural current.

---

# Part CXXIX — Failure mode 18: "Closer sensors automatically solve source localization"

Why it fails:

Higher SNR helps, but nonuniqueness, calibration, interference, and modeling remain.

---

# Part CXXX — Failure mode 19: "A dipole fit proves there was one dipole"

Why it fails:

ECD is an effective model of a compact population source.

---

# Part CXXXI — Failure mode 20: "A distributed source map is assumption-free"

Why it fails:

Regularization and priors select one solution among many.

---

# Part CXXXII — Failure mode 21: "Beamformers reveal exact local activity"

Why it fails:

Weights depend on covariance, lead fields, regularization, and source correlations.

---

# Part CXXXIII — Failure mode 22: "Source-space correlation proves communication"

Why it fails:

Leakage and common sources can create correlation.

---

# Part CXXXIV — Failure mode 23: "Evoked averaging shows all task activity"

Why it fails:

Non-phase-locked activity can cancel.

---

# Part CXXXV — Failure mode 24: "Millisecond temporal resolution means millimeter spatial certainty"

Why it fails:

Temporal and spatial uncertainty are different.

---

# Part CXXXVI — Failure mode 25: "Deep sources are impossible"

Why it fails:

Sensitivity generally decreases with depth, but "impossible" is too absolute.

---

# Part CXXXVII — Failure mode 26: "A weak field means weak total neural activity"

Why it fails:

Orientation, distance, extent, and cancellation can reduce measured field.

---

# Part CXXXVIII — Failure mode 27: "Head movement is only a motion artifact"

Why it fails:

It also changes the source-sensor geometry and therefore the lead field.

---

# Part CXXXIX — Failure mode 28: "Coordinates are bookkeeping"

Why it fails:

Wrong coordinate transforms produce wrong anatomical localization.

---

# Part CXL — Failure mode 29: "More source-map decimals mean more localization accuracy"

Why it fails:

Numerical precision can greatly exceed physical accuracy.

---

# Part CXLI — Failure mode 30: "The same preprocessing is safe for every analysis"

Why it fails:

Filtering, artifact removal, covariance estimation, and trial selection interact with the target phenomenon.

# Compact summary

```text
1. MEG measures magnetic fields outside the head generated by neural electric currents.

2. MEG does not measure membrane voltage, a cortical image, or one uniquely localized source.

3. The relevant magnetic fields are extraordinarily small, often in the fT–pT range.

4. Synchronized postsynaptic and intracellular currents in aligned cortical populations dominate conventional MEG.

5. An equivalent current dipole is an effective source model, not a literal anatomical object.

6. Source orientation strongly affects MEG sensitivity.

7. In the ideal spherical model, tangential dipoles are visible and purely radial dipoles are externally silent; real heads make this a useful approximation rather than an absolute law.

8. MEG is generally less distorted by skull conductivity boundaries than scalp EEG, but head geometry and volume currents still matter.

9. A MEG sensor measures one component or spatial difference of a distributed magnetic field.

10. Magnetometers and gradiometers have different sensitivity and interference-rejection properties.

11. Neural MEG is tiny relative to environmental magnetic fields, motivating shielding and interference suppression.

12. Conventional MEG commonly uses cryogenic SQUID sensors.

13. OPM-MEG uses noncryogenic atomic magnetometers that can be placed closer to the scalp and arranged in wearable arrays.

14. OPMs change system constraints but not the neural source physics.

15. Sensor-space field maps are not cortical source maps.

16. Head position, digitization, coordinate transforms, and MRI coregistration are part of source modeling.

17. The MEG forward model is approximately linear: b = L j + noise.

18. The inverse problem is nonunique; source localization therefore requires constraints or priors.

19. ECD fitting, minimum-norm estimation, and beamforming make different assumptions.

20. Source leakage can create false spatial spread and apparent connectivity.

21. MEG has millisecond-scale temporal resolution, but spatial accuracy is not one fixed number.

22. MEG and EEG are complementary electromagnetic measurements of related neural sources.

23. MEG is not MRI; MRI is often added for anatomy.

24. MEG is more electrophysiologically direct than hemodynamic fMRI but still requires model-based interpretation.

25. Biological artifacts, environmental fields, metal, and movement can greatly exceed brain signals.

26. A weak measured MEG field does not necessarily mean weak neural activity because orientation, distance, depth, extent, and cancellation all matter.

27. Reproducible MEG requires sensor, geometry, timing, preprocessing, forward-model, inverse-model, and coordinate metadata.

28. The next lesson moves to fMRI BOLD and the logic of hemodynamic inference.
```
