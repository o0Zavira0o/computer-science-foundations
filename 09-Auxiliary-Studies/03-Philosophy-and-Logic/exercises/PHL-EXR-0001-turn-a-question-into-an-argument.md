# PHL-EXR-0001 — Turn a Question into an Argument

**Track:** Philosophy and Logic
**Companion lesson:** [`PHL-0001`](../lessons/PHL-0001-what-philosophy-and-logic-are-actually-doing.md)

## Goal

Practice the smallest complete philosophical workflow: question → claim → reason → objection → revision.

## Part A — choose a question

Pick one:

1. Is it ever rational to believe something you cannot personally verify?
2. Can a person remain the same person after radical psychological change?
3. Does a good outcome justify breaking a fair rule?
4. Could an artificial system have interests of its own?

You may substitute another genuine philosophical question.

## Part B — write a candidate claim

Write **one sentence** that could be true or false.

Avoid answers such as:

```text
It depends.
It's complicated.
Everyone has their own opinion.
```

Those may signal complexity, but they do not yet state a position that can be examined.

## Part C — give one reason

Write one reason that, if accepted, would make the claim more credible.

Then ask:

> Is this actually a reason for my claim, or did I merely restate the claim in different words?

## Part D — build the strongest objection you can

Do not deliberately invent a weak opponent.

Try one of these strategies:

- challenge a premise;
- produce a counterexample;
- expose an ambiguity;
- show an unwanted consequence;
- introduce relevant evidence;
- propose a rival explanation.

## Part E — revise

Choose one response:

```text
Keep the original claim and defend it.
Narrow the claim.
Add a qualification.
Replace one premise.
Abandon the claim.
```

Write why.

## Argument card

Your finished artifact should fit this template:

```text
Question:
Candidate claim:
Reason:
Objection:
Revision / defense:
What evidence or further distinction would help next:
```

## Transfer challenge

Take the statement:

> “A sufficiently intelligent AI would obviously be conscious.”

Rewrite it into a form that exposes at least two hidden questions or assumptions.

<details>
<summary>Possible direction</summary>

You might separate:

- what “intelligent” means;
- what “conscious” means;
- whether intelligence is sufficient for consciousness;
- what evidence would justify attributing consciousness;
- whether behavioral evidence and implementation evidence play different roles.

The point is not to endorse a theory of AI consciousness. It is to turn a compressed assertion into analyzable questions and premises.

</details>

## Completion criterion

You should finish with a position that is **more precise after criticism than before it**.

If the exercise only made you more confident in your starting opinion, try again with a stronger objection.
